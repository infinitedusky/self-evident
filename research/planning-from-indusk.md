# Planning: InDusk's approach, ported

Read-only study of InDusk's planner (`apps/indusk-mcp/skills/planner.md`, 801 lines, and the real plans under
`.indusk/planning/`), 2026-10-21. Sandy: the approach was right; the file-based process turned into an app made it
slow. On the new foundation the brief is a row, the planner is an agent run, and what it produces is rows.

## Vocabulary (decided 2026-10-21)

**idea → plan → task → phase → step.** An idea is one task or a plan of several. A plan is tasks in order (an
InDusk master plan). A task is authored: title + brief (an InDusk plan). Phases are generated by the planner from
the task's brief: the atomic unit the engine schedules, one agent / one worktree / one commit, each with files and
depends-on (an InDusk phase). Steps are the builder's own todo list while it builds a phase.

**Promises stay, the registry goes.** InDusk's mistake was making the promise the required way of telling the
agent what to build; the brief does that. A promise is what must hold, mapped directly to OpenTelemetry: a
**clause** is a span written as true/false (the promise asserting itself inside the running app); a **sensor** is
a span that measures something, for a test or a monitor. Evidence and checks point at clauses and sensors.

## What InDusk did

A six-document pipeline, research → brief → test-plan → ADR → impl → retrospective, each with a person sign-off,
and a cold `plan-review.md` before approval. Workflow type (`feature|bugfix|refactor|spike`) picked the documents.

### Worth keeping verbatim: the conversation

> "Ask what should be true when this is done, in their words: 'What should this do for the people who use it?',
> 'What is wrong today?', 'Is there anything you have already decided, or want to avoid?' Ask why as well: what
> they expect to follow from it. Settle what will be true, not how to build it: an approach offered before the
> promises exist anchors the conversation on the code."

> "**Say it back.** Put what you heard as promises, one plain sentence each about what will be true and never
> about how, and as expectations, each with how you would know and when to look. Say them back and take
> corrections until the person agrees. Nothing is saved yet."

> "**How many plans.** Promises whose work shares code are one plan … Promises that share none are separate
> plans under a master. Suggest the grouping and why, then let the person decide."

Plus: go through every existing promise the work comes near — still true / changes / replaces.

### Worth keeping: the decision shape (`src/tools/decision-tools.ts`)

`question`, `options[] {label, description?, changes?: (brief|promise|look|gate)[]}` (at least two), `recommended`,
`reason`, `cost: cheap|moderate|expensive`.

> "Ask the person a choice the plan does not settle … Give at least two options, the one you recommend and why,
> and a cost: cheap (easy to undo), moderate, or expensive. For each option say what it changes — brief, promise,
> look or gate — when it changes what was agreed. Never decide such a choice silently."

The **server** fixes the cost, never the agent: any option that declares `changes` is raised to `expensive`.
States `waiting → answered | went-ahead → answered-late`; a late answer that disagrees opens a follow-on, it
doesn't block. "A choice you settle from the plan itself needs no question: write it and its reason into the plan
and carry on." Decided-unattended without a question id was refused.

### Worth keeping: the phase (`run-the-graph/impl.md`)

```
### Build Phase 1: The plan reads as a graph, and the gate hook judges by it
**Depends on**: none
**Files**: apps/indusk-mcp/src/lib/graph/declarations.ts, …
{Summary for someone who has worked here but never read this plan}
- [ ] step … <details><summary>Technical</summary>…</details>
#### Verification  — behavioural checks with the command and the expected result
```

Median plan: 5–6 phases. Cut by what each phase produces and consumes ("granular enough to track, not so granular
they're busywork"). One phase per worktree, cut from the plan branch, joined by checked fast-forward (designed,
approved, never built there; built here as the engine).

### Worth keeping: the brief's shape, not its ceremony

Expectations (each with how you'd know and when to look), promises (one plain sentence each, what will be true),
existing promises (must not break / changes / replaces), not promised, looks or "none, because". The best briefs
also had "the end state, in stories". Acceptance meant the person said so *and* a registry contract held; the
registry is what made it slow (26 token refusals at one close).

### Worth keeping from the ADR and the review

The numbered decision list with its "Rejected: …" tail (alternatives recorded, not lost). From the cold review,
three questions were worth one pass: *Does the impl build what each promise claims, and nothing it does not? What
else would have to change for each promise to hold? What does the code do that the brief never promised?*

## What made it slow (drop consciously)

The hash-chained review: any fix staled the hash, a stale review forced a full cold re-read, a re-read found a
new set (41 → 28 → 19 findings then four rechecks on one plan; 57 findings and 15 failed rechecks on the one that
stalled). Clauses-as-document-structure, five test levels, the trajectory table, four gates per phase, three
close-out rituals, the promise registry and `// promise:` tokens, document lifecycle states, re-approval on
amendment, and ~5,000 lines of hooks enforcing document shape (`check-gates.js`, `validate-impl-structure.js`,
`plan-write-guard.js`, …). The test-plan's behavioural-wording rule earned its keep ("read the assertion aloud to a
non-engineer"); its scaffolding did not.

## The port: one planner run, two stops

**Beat 1, the brief (stop: accept).** The conversation protocol above, in the UI or CLI, until the person agrees.
Rows: `task.brief` (and for a plan of several, the plan's spec), `promise[]` as prose for now.

**Beat 2, the phases (stop: approve, or just run).** One agent run with read-only tools on the repo and the task's
brief, returning one validated object (Agent SDK `outputFormat: json_schema`):

- `decisions[] {n, title, chosen, rejected[], reason}` — the ADR's D-list
- `questions[] {question, options[{label, description, changes}], recommended, reason}` — cost set by the server
- `phases[] {key, title, summary, depends_on[], files[], steps[{text, technical}], checks[{statement, command, expected}], done_when}`
- `notes` — what it assumed

Then one self-review pass with the three questions above, applied as edits in the same run. No hash, no re-read.
Rows: `phase` + `phase_dependency` + `phase_file_claim` (via `addPhases`), `decision`, `question`. The person sees
the graph, the decision cards (Recommended marked, cost shown), and the questions; they edit or run.

**Better than Lightsprint's plan mode:** say-it-back before any approach; `changes` on options with the cost raised
by the server; nothing decided silently; late answers open a follow-on instead of blocking; phase summaries for a
reader without the plan's shorthand; depends-on and files per phase; rejected options recorded.
